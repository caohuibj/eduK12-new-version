# K–12 teacher feedback literacy | 2025 English reflection edition

![A teacher and a student look together at two versions of a piece of work.](../art/cover.webp)

A useful comment often becomes clear only when a learner tries to act on it. This reflection helps you connect something you noticed in class with one change you can try next time. Choose a recent lesson and work through the sections that fit it.

## Before you start

For a prompt that fits, note an example, a question or something you want to learn. A few words are enough. Leave any prompt blank or decline it. If you mention student work, leave out names, images and other identifying details.

## Knowledge

Ask what makes a piece of feedback useful

Take one actual comment before comparing theories. Was it about the goal, the work so far, or a way to move forward? The example helps you decide what to read or discuss next.

Find a short piece of feedback you remember writing. Underline the part that tells the learner how to improve. If that part is hard to find, try adding one plain sentence.

## Skills

Leave room for a learner to respond

Recall how you gave a comment and what the learner could do with it. Timing, wording and the chance to try again all matter. A specific recollection will tell you more than a broad judgement of your skill.

Give one short next-step suggestion, then provide time for a second attempt. Compare the two attempts to see which part of the comment the learner used.

## Values

Find out how feedback is heard

Think of a moment when feedback changed the tone of a lesson. A learner might feel able to ask for help or think their work is already settled. Your observations help you understand what the feedback made possible.

Ask a learner to explain one thing they understood and one thing they still wondered about. Use their words to revisit how you offered the original feedback.

## Actionability

Make the next step easy to find

A comment can sound clear to the person who wrote it and still leave a learner unsure where to begin. Looking at what happens after the comment helps you find the missing step.

Replace one general comment with a small task the learner can do now. Ask them to explain the step in their own words, then check what changed in their work.

## After your reflection

Choose one change to a comment or classroom routine. Decide what you will look for in the next attempt. Come back to that example before changing several things at once.

A blank prompt tells you what was left unwritten on this occasion. It gives no information about the teacher’s knowledge or practice. Reflections are kept as text and have no score bands or automated interpretation.
